Naming is a measurement problem.

Most firms name things by taste. We name them by instrument: seventy-plus candidates on plates, screened through three thresholds anyone in the room can read, and the winner is the name that survives the most readings — not the one that won the meeting.

The industry treats naming as a creative event. It is not. It is a measurement problem wearing a creative costume, and the costume is where budgets die. A name is a claim about relevance, distinctiveness, and load — and all three can be held against tolerance, the way any machinist holds a part.

What follows is the office method, verbatim. It has been used on nine systems programs since 2023 and has never missed a trademark office without a plate to show for it.

Screening starts mechanical. Every candidate runs the same three reads — TM, tone, and tongue — before anyone discusses whether they “like” it. Likes are recorded; they are not arguments.

The plate tool that produces the reading sheet is forty lines of Python and never leaves the office. Its output is deliberately plain, because the point of a reading sheet is that it cannot be argued with:

# plate.py — press a name to the reading sheet
def screen(candidates, corpus):
    for name in candidates:
        tm   = tm_threshold(name, corpus)
        tone = tone_threshold(name, position)
        load = load_threshold(name)
        if tm and tone and load:
            yield name, "pressed", {
                "tm":   round(tm, 2),
                "tone": round(tone, 2),
                "load": round(load, 2),
            }
        else:
            yield name, "rejected", why(tm, tone, load)

Every rejected name keeps its why string. The rejection record is the method's second product: boards learn that a verdict comes with evidence, which shortens every naming argument that follows. Press ⌘ K in the Ledger to reproduce any screen.

The bench · four plates, two survive

Plate benches are printed weekly during a naming program. A candidate only reaches a plate after clearing the mechanical screens — which is why the bench rarely carries more than four names. The client never sees a name in a vacuum; they see it at width, on the shelf, and beside the competitor.

Gate 3 — the tongue threshold

Recall testing is run on unaided plates: the candidate is read aloud by nine strangers, once, no context, and the transcript is scored. Anything below a strict passing band on the first read does not proceed — no matter how a room feels about it.

The verdict that matters is written by a stranger with no stake in the room, once, and scored. Everything else in naming is commentary.
