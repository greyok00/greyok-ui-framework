The 41-minute brownout

Severity SEV-2 · 61% of pdx-1 readers affected · no data loss · resolved 14:52 PT

A DNS change nobody approved — a preview environment's nameservers leaking into production — sent 61% of our US reader traffic on a 3-second detour through Singapore. This is how it happened, what it cost, and the three changes that make it not happen again.

What readers saw

From 14:11 to 14:52 PT, page weight felt normal but every first byte took 2.9–3.4 seconds. Dashboards looked healthy the whole time — p99 latency rose 18×, but CPU, memory and error counts didn't move, so every threshold-based alert stayed dark. Our own anomaly detector caught it in 6 minutes; a human confirmed it in 4 more.

the tell

TCP was fine. DNS was not.

Latency tripled while error rate stayed at 0.04%. That signature — fast TCP, slow names — is exactly what the anomaly detector now watches for, because a threshold on any single metric here would have stayed silent.

The part we got wrong by design

Our review tooling let a change in the dns.zone file ride along with an application feature flag because the diff line count was small. Review fatigue is real: median review size that week was 4 lines. Small diffs get shallow review, so zone files now require a second, structural review regardless of diff size.

The mitigation playbook now pins nameservers per region, and the preview/prod boundary moved into infrastructure: nameserver sets are declared once, and the deployer literally cannot mint a new set outside the staging namespace. The TTL on prod NS records dropped from 300s to 60s — the brownout class of incident now self-resolves in minutes, without a human.
